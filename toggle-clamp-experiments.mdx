A clamp that springs open during a cut ruins the part and can injure the operator. A clamp that needs two hands to close slows every cycle. A toggle clamp avoids both: it presses the work with more force than the hand gives, and then it stays closed with no hand on it. Both effects come from one idea: a linkage that is pushed just past the position where two of its links form a straight line. In these six experiments you find that position on a simulator, measure how the force rises and falls near it, check how well the linkage pushes, size the pins and links, and choose a clamp for a real job. #ToggleClamp #MechanismDesign #ForceAnalysis

## How a toggle clamp works

A toggle clamp presses a pad onto a workpiece through two links that a handle pushes into a straight line, like a knee: a bent knee needs a strong muscle to carry you, and a straight knee needs almost none. Locking pliers, a toolbox latch, and the clamps that hold a mould closed work the same way.

```text
     open               dead centre            locked

       A
      / \                                 O-----.     .-----B
     /   \           O------A------B             '-A-'
    O     B                                        ^
                                                   |
  links bent:        links in line:        A is past the line
  small force        greatest force        and rests on a stop
```

When the handle link (O to A) and the main link (A to B) are almost in line, a large movement of the handle moves the end B only a little. Work in equals work out, so that small movement comes with a large force: the force multiplication is greatest just before the links form a straight line. That position is dead centre, which the simulator calls top-dead-centre (TDC).

At TDC the clamp is not yet locked, because a small shake can bend the links back. So the handle goes a few degrees further, to a stop. Now the push of the workpiece presses the handle into the stop, and the clamp holds with no hand on it.

For a perfect toggle of two equal links, the mechanical advantage (MA, output force divided by input force) is one divided by twice the tangent of the toggle angle $\alpha$, the angle of each link from the straight line:

$$
\text{MA} = \frac{1}{2\tan\alpha}
$$

That is a limit. A real clamp has pin friction and unequal links, so the simulator shows less: at 5 degrees past centre the formula gives 5.7, and the simulator's medium-duty clamp gives about 1.5.

:::caution[Simulator only]
A closed clamp holds stored force, and a handle released near centre can fly back. Find the limits on the simulator, never by loading a real clamp until something gives.
:::

## Reference

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Lock margin** | Degrees the handle rests past TDC against a stop |
| **$\alpha$ (toggle angle)** | Angle of the handle link from the line of the main link; zero at TDC. In the real clamp it is larger than the lock margin |
| **$\mu$ (transmission angle)** | Angle between the main link and the clamp arm where they join; below about 40 degrees the link mostly presses on the pin |
| **$\phi$ (friction angle)** | $\arctan$ of the pin friction coefficient $f$; friction acts like an angle added to $\alpha$ |
| **$S_y$, $N$** | Yield strength (the stress at which a part bends permanently) and safety factor; allowable stress $= S_y / N$ |

## Experiment 1: Top-Dead-Centre and Self-Locking

<InArticleAd />


Close a toggle clamp and the handle passes a hard point, then stays down by itself. That point is TDC. If the stop is set before it, the clamp opens when the hand leaves. This experiment finds TDC and shows why the handle must rest a few degrees past it.

:::note[Objective]
Find the top-dead-centre handle angle for the medium-duty preset, see how the mechanical advantage rises toward TDC, and explain why a small lock margin past TDC makes the clamp hold by itself.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Configure the simulator**
   Open the simulator and select the Medium-duty vertical preset. Note the info bar shows the TDC handle angle. Keep "Show parameter cues" on so you can see the TDC reference line on the diagram.

2. **Predict**
   The ideal relation $\text{MA} = \dfrac{1}{2\tan\alpha}$ grows without limit as $\alpha$ goes to zero. Predict that the simulator's chart rises toward TDC in the same way but stays finite.

3. **Sweep the handle**
   Drag the handle-angle slider slowly from open toward the locked stop. The MA in the info bar should rise as the teal current-angle line meets the red TDC line on the charts, and fall after it has crossed.

4. **Record values near TDC**
   Using the lock-margin slider, set the lock margin to 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 degrees. At each setting read the engineering MA and the clamping force at lock from the panel.

5. **Save data**
   Create `data/exp1_tdc.csv` with columns: lock_margin_deg, MA_sim, Fpad_sim.
</Steps>

### Python Analysis

```python title="experiment_1_tdc.py"
# Toggle Clamp Experiment 1: Top-Dead-Centre and Self-Locking
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import os

os.makedirs('plots', exist_ok=True)

# Ideal toggle relation: MA = 1 / (2 tan(alpha)), alpha = toggle angle past TDC
alpha_deg = np.linspace(1, 40, 300)
alpha = np.deg2rad(alpha_deg)
MA_ideal = 1.0 / (2.0 * np.tan(alpha))

# Load simulator readings if present (lock margin ~ toggle angle alpha)
# The ideal curve is the limit for a perfect toggle: expect the simulator points well below it.
csv_path = 'data/exp1_tdc.csv'
sim = np.genfromtxt(csv_path, delimiter=',', skip_header=1) if os.path.exists(csv_path) else None

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 5))
plt.plot(alpha_deg, MA_ideal, '-', color='#2A9D8F', linewidth=2,
         label='Ideal toggle: MA = 1/(2 tan a)')
if sim is not None:
    plt.plot(sim[:, 0], sim[:, 1], 'o', color='#ef4444', markersize=6, label='Simulator (engineering)')
plt.axvline(0, color='#ef4444', linestyle='--', alpha=0.6)
plt.text(0.5, plt.ylim()[1] * 0.8, 'TDC', color='#ef4444')
plt.xlabel('Toggle angle past TDC, alpha (deg)')
plt.ylabel('Mechanical advantage')
plt.title('Force amplification diverges at top-dead-centre')
plt.ylim(0, 20)
plt.legend()
plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('plots/exp1_tdc.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()

for a in [2, 5, 10]:
    print(f"alpha={a:2d} deg -> ideal MA = {1/(2*np.tan(np.deg2rad(a))):.2f}")
print("As alpha -> 0 (TDC), MA -> infinity: a tiny push holds a large load,")
print("so resting a few degrees PAST TDC against a stop makes the clamp self-lock.")
```

### Expected Results

- The ideal MA rises without limit as the toggle angle approaches zero
- The simulator's engineering MA has the same shape but peaks at about 5.5 at TDC, because pin friction and efficiency limit it
- At lock margins of 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 degrees the simulator gives about 3.05, 1.84, 1.28, 0.95, 0.74, and 0.60. At 5 degrees it gives 1.5 (182 N from 120 N at the handle), where the ideal relation gives 5.7
- Past about 8 degrees the pad presses with less force than the hand gives
- Past TDC the push of the workpiece turns the handle toward the stop, so the clamp locks itself

### Design Question

If you wanted the clamp to hold more securely with no extra hand effort, would you set the lock margin to 3 degrees or 9 degrees, and what do you give up by choosing the larger margin?

## Experiment 2: Force Amplification with Friction

<InArticleAd />


The ideal relation promises unlimited force at TDC. Friction in the pins takes much of it, and dry or worn pins take more, so a clamp sized from the ideal relation disappoints. This experiment compares the ideal relation, a friction model, and the simulator.

:::note[Objective]
Compare the ideal toggle MA with a friction model and the simulator's engineering model, and measure how much pin friction and efficiency reduce the clamping force.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Configure the simulator**
   Select the Medium-duty preset. Note the handle force, pin friction, and efficiency in the Forces and material panel.

2. **Predict**
   Friction acts like an extra angle, the friction angle $\phi = \arctan f$ for a pin friction coefficient $f$, so the friction model $\text{MA} = \dfrac{1}{2\tan(\alpha + \phi)}$ stays finite at TDC. It still describes a perfect two-link toggle. Predict whether friction changes it more or less than it changes the simulator's real linkage.

3. **Vary friction**
   Set pin friction to 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, and 0.20. At each value, read the engineering MA and clamping force at the locked position.

4. **Vary efficiency**
   Reset friction to 0.12. Set efficiency to 0.6, 0.75, and 0.9 and record the clamping force at lock.

5. **Save data**
   Create `data/exp2_friction.csv` with columns: pin_friction, MA_sim, Fpad_sim.
</Steps>

### Python Analysis

```python title="experiment_2_friction.py"
# Toggle Clamp Experiment 2: Force Amplification with Friction
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import os

os.makedirs('plots', exist_ok=True)

F_handle = 120.0   # N
eta = 0.76         # mechanism efficiency
alpha_deg = np.linspace(2, 40, 300)
alpha = np.deg2rad(alpha_deg)

MA_ideal = 1.0 / (2.0 * np.tan(alpha))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13, 5))

# Left: MA models for one friction value
# mu is the pin friction coefficient here (f in the text), not the transmission angle
mu = 0.12
phi = np.arctan(mu)
MA_friction = 1.0 / (2.0 * np.tan(alpha + phi))
MA_eng = eta * MA_friction
axes[0].plot(alpha_deg, MA_ideal, '-', color='#2A9D8F', lw=2, label='Ideal')
axes[0].plot(alpha_deg, MA_friction, '-', color='#E9C46A', lw=2, label=f'Friction (mu={mu})')
axes[0].plot(alpha_deg, MA_eng, '-', color='#ef4444', lw=2, label=f'Engineering (eta={eta})')
axes[0].set_xlabel('Toggle angle alpha (deg)')
axes[0].set_ylabel('Mechanical advantage')
axes[0].set_title('Friction and efficiency cap the amplification')
axes[0].set_ylim(0, 15)
axes[0].legend(); axes[0].grid(True, alpha=0.3)

# Right: peak achievable MA vs friction (evaluated at a 5 deg lock margin)
mus = np.linspace(0.05, 0.20, 50)
a_lock = np.deg2rad(5.0)
peak = eta / (2.0 * np.tan(a_lock + np.arctan(mus)))
axes[1].plot(mus, peak, '-', color='#f97316', lw=2)
csv_path = 'data/exp2_friction.csv'
if os.path.exists(csv_path):
    sim = np.genfromtxt(csv_path, delimiter=',', skip_header=1)
    axes[1].plot(sim[:, 0], sim[:, 1], 'o', color='#2A9D8F', ms=6, label='Simulator')
    axes[1].legend()
axes[1].set_xlabel('Pin friction coefficient mu')
axes[1].set_ylabel('MA at 5 deg lock margin')
axes[1].set_title('Higher friction means less amplification')
axes[1].grid(True, alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('plots/exp2_friction.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()

for mu in [0.05, 0.12, 0.20]:
    ma = eta / (2 * np.tan(a_lock + np.arctan(mu)))
    print(f"mu={mu:.2f}: MA={ma:.2f}, F_pad={ma*F_handle:.0f} N at 5 deg lock margin")
```

### Expected Results

- In the simulator at the 5-degree lock, raising pin friction from 0.05 to 0.20 lowers the clamping force from about 209 N to 158 N (a quarter). The two-link model in the script falls from 330 N to 156 N (half)
- Efficiency from 0.9 to 0.6 lowers the simulator's force from about 198 N to 159 N: 20 percent, not a third, because the friction limit acts in both cases
- The simulator lies below the two-link model at low friction and meets it near 0.20. Both are far below the ideal curve

### Design Question

A supplier offers low-friction bushings that drop the pin friction coefficient from 0.15 to 0.08. For the medium-duty clamp at a 5-degree lock margin, roughly how much more clamping force would you gain, and is it worth a higher per-unit cost for a hand-operated clamp?

## Experiment 3: Transmission Angle and Geometry Quality

<InArticleAd />


A large force in the main link is useful only if it turns the clamp arm. The transmission angle, between the main link and the clamp arm, measures that: below about 40 degrees most of the push presses on the pin (the same rule as in the [four-bar linkage experiments](/education/mechanism-design-simulation/four-bar-linkage-experiments/)). The medium-duty clamp is far below 40 degrees at lock and still holds. This experiment shows why, and what the low angle costs.

:::note[Objective]
Map the transmission angle across the handle stroke, measure how much of the travel is below the 40-degree limit, and explain why a toggle clamp still works with a small transmission angle at its lock point.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Configure the simulator**
   Select the Medium-duty preset. Read the link lengths from the Geometry panel: handle $L_h$, main link $L_m$, arm $L_{\text{arm}}$, base spacing $d$.

2. **Predict**
   The transmission angle is smallest where the main link and the clamp arm come closest to one line. Predict whether the locked position keeps the angle above 40 degrees.

3. **Read the transmission-angle chart**
   Open the Transmission angle chart. Note the dashed 40-degree limit line, the high value when open, and where the curve sits at the locked position.

4. **Change geometry**
   Increase the main link length by 30 percent, then the arm length, and watch how the transmission-angle curve shifts. Record the value at lock for each change.

5. **Save data**
   Download the analysis CSV from the simulator, keep the `handle_angle_deg` and `transmission_angle_deg` columns, and save them as `data/exp3_transmission.csv`. Note the values at open, at TDC, and at lock.
</Steps>

### Python Analysis

```python title="experiment_3_transmission.py"
# Toggle Clamp Experiment 3: Transmission Angle and Geometry Quality
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import os

os.makedirs('plots', exist_ok=True)

# Preferred: use the simulator's own transmission angle. From the downloaded CSV
# (siliconwit-toggle-clamp-data.csv) keep two columns and save as the file below:
#   handle_angle_deg, transmission_angle_deg
csv_path = 'data/exp3_transmission.csv'

if os.path.exists(csv_path):
    d = np.genfromtxt(csv_path, delimiter=',', skip_header=1)
    handle, mu = d[:, 0], d[:, 1]
    source = 'simulator data'
else:
    # Fallback analytical four-bar transmission angle (law of cosines) so the script
    # still runs without the CSV. Medium-duty link lengths (mm).
    d_g, Lh, Lm, Larm = 70.0, 40.0, 80.0, 55.0
    handle = np.linspace(20, 120, 300)
    q = np.sqrt(d_g**2 + Lh**2 - 2 * d_g * Lh * np.cos(np.deg2rad(handle)))
    cosg = np.clip((Lm**2 + Larm**2 - q**2) / (2 * Lm * Larm), -1, 1)
    mu = np.degrees(np.arccos(cosg))
    mu = np.where(mu > 90, 180 - mu, mu)
    source = 'analytical (law of cosines)'

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 5))
plt.plot(handle, mu, '-', color='#2A9D8F', lw=2, label=f'Transmission angle ({source})')
plt.axhline(40, color='#ef4444', linestyle='--', label='Classic four-bar limit (40 deg)')
plt.xlabel('Handle angle (deg)')
plt.ylabel('Transmission angle (deg)')
plt.title('Transmission angle collapses near the over-centre lock')
plt.legend(); plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('plots/exp3_transmission.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()

print(f"Source: {source}")
print(f"Transmission angle range: {np.nanmin(mu):.1f} to {np.nanmax(mu):.1f} deg")
print(f"Fraction of stroke below 40 deg: {100*np.nanmean(mu < 40):.0f}%")
print("It is high when the clamp is open and small near the lock in this preset. A small")
print("transmission angle costs force: the force gain comes from the handle and toggle link")
print("coming into one line, not from the low angle. A clamp can accept it at the lock")
print("because it only has to hold there, where a turning four-bar has to keep moving.")
```

### Expected Results

- The transmission angle is high when the clamp is open (about 77 degrees for the medium-duty preset) and falls steadily as the handle moves toward top-dead-centre
- At lock it is near 16 degrees, and roughly 40 percent of the stroke is below 40 degrees
- The clamp still holds, because nothing moves at lock: the stop keeps it closed, and the 40-degree rule is for links that move a load
- The low angle costs force. For rigid links the MA is proportional to $\sin\mu / \sin\alpha$: the gain comes from a small toggle angle $\alpha$, and a small $\mu$ takes part of it back ($\sin 16^\circ = 0.28$). The light-duty and heavy-duty presets lock near 30 degrees
- Keep the transmission angle high through the approach, where the clamping bar travels

### Design Question

The medium-duty clamp locks at a transmission angle near 16 degrees. Which link length would you change to raise that angle toward 30 degrees, and what happens to the TDC position and the handle travel when you do?

## Experiment 4: Pin and Link Stress Sizing

<InArticleAd />


The force inside a toggle clamp is far larger than the force at the pad: 120 N at the handle of the medium-duty clamp puts about 3200 N into the main link at lock. A pin cut through by that force lets the handle fly back. The pin is in shear (the force tries to cut it across), and the link is pulled and bent. This experiment sizes both against an allowable stress, the simulator's Safe or Over-stressed check.

:::note[Objective]
Compute pin shear stress and link bending stress for the clamping load, compare them with the allowable stress from a yield strength and safety factor, and find the smallest safe pin diameter and link section.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Configure the simulator**
   Select the Medium-duty preset. In the Material strength panel set yield strength to 350 MPa and safety factor to 2. Open the Link and pin stress chart and note the dashed allowable lines and the sizing verdict.

2. **Predict**
   Shear stress is the link force divided by the pin's cross-section area, which grows with the square of the diameter $d$. So the stress goes as $1/d^2$: twice the diameter, a quarter of the stress. Predict the smallest pin diameter that stays below the shear allowable.

3. **Shrink the pin**
   Reduce the pin diameter step by step from 10 mm to 4 mm and watch the verdict flip from Safe to Over-stressed. Record the diameter at the transition.

4. **Shrink the link**
   Reset the pin, then reduce link thickness and width until the bending stress exceeds its allowable. Record the section at the transition.

5. **Save data**
   Create `data/exp4_stress.csv` with columns: pin_diameter_mm, pin_shear_MPa, verdict.
</Steps>

### Python Analysis

```python title="experiment_4_stress.py"
# Toggle Clamp Experiment 4: Pin and Link Stress Sizing
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import os

os.makedirs('plots', exist_ok=True)

# Representative link force at lock (N) from the simulator, and material allowable
F_link = 3200.0      # N (read the pin-reaction / link-force magnitude at lock)
Sy = 350.0           # MPa yield
N = 2.0              # safety factor
sigma_allow = Sy / N
tau_allow = 0.5 * Sy / N

# Pin shear stress vs diameter: tau = F / (pi d^2 / 4)
d = np.linspace(3, 20, 300)               # mm
tau = F_link / (np.pi * d**2 / 4)         # MPa (N/mm^2)

# Link bending stress vs square section side s (t = w = s): axial + bending with e = d/2
s = np.linspace(4, 24, 300)
d_pin = 8.0
A = s * s
I = s * s**3 / 12
sigma = F_link / A + (F_link * (d_pin / 2) * (s / 2)) / I

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13, 5))
axes[0].plot(d, tau, '-', color='#E9C46A', lw=2, label='Pin shear')
axes[0].axhline(tau_allow, color='#ef4444', ls='--', label=f'Allowable tau = {tau_allow:.0f} MPa')
d_min = d[np.where(tau <= tau_allow)[0][0]]
axes[0].axvline(d_min, color='#2A9D8F', ls=':', label=f'min safe d ~ {d_min:.1f} mm')
axes[0].set_xlabel('Pin diameter (mm)'); axes[0].set_ylabel('Shear stress (MPa)')
axes[0].set_title('Pin shear ~ 1/d^2'); axes[0].set_ylim(0, 3 * tau_allow)
axes[0].legend(); axes[0].grid(True, alpha=0.3)

axes[1].plot(s, sigma, '-', color='#2A9D8F', lw=2, label='Link bending+axial')
axes[1].axhline(sigma_allow, color='#ef4444', ls='--', label=f'Allowable sigma = {sigma_allow:.0f} MPa')
s_min = s[np.where(sigma <= sigma_allow)[0][0]]
axes[1].axvline(s_min, color='#E9C46A', ls=':', label=f'min safe side ~ {s_min:.1f} mm')
axes[1].set_xlabel('Square link side (mm)'); axes[1].set_ylabel('Stress (MPa)')
axes[1].set_title('Link stress falls with section size'); axes[1].set_ylim(0, 3 * sigma_allow)
axes[1].legend(); axes[1].grid(True, alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('plots/exp4_stress.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()

print(f"Allowable: sigma={sigma_allow:.0f} MPa, tau={tau_allow:.0f} MPa")
print(f"Smallest safe pin diameter ~ {d_min:.1f} mm")
print(f"Smallest safe square link side ~ {s_min:.1f} mm")
```

### Expected Results

- With yield 350 MPa and a safety factor of 2 the allowables are 175 MPa (bending) and about 88 MPa (shear)
- Pin shear stress falls steeply with diameter. The smallest diameter below the shear allowable is about 6.9 mm for a link force of 3200 N, which is where the simulator's verdict flips to Over-stressed
- Link stress falls with section size. Thickness matters more than width, because the bending part of the stress falls with the square of the thickness
- The script sizes a square link (smallest safe side about 8.4 mm). The simulator's 6 x 22 mm link stays Safe down to a thickness of about 5 mm

### Design Question

You can make the clamp safe either by increasing the pin diameter or by upgrading to a 500 MPa alloy steel. Which change gives more margin for the same added cost, and why might a designer still prefer the larger pin?

## Experiment 5: Lock-Margin Trade-Offs

<InArticleAd />


The lock margin is how far past TDC the handle rests. With a small margin the clamp presses hard, but vibration can work the handle back through centre and open it. With a large margin it locks firmly but presses with less force. This experiment maps that trade.

:::note[Objective]
Measure how the clamping force and the security of the lock change with lock margin, and choose a margin that balances holding force against release effort.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Configure the simulator**
   Select the Medium-duty preset. Keep handle force and friction fixed.

2. **Predict**
   The two-link friction model with efficiency $\eta$, $\text{MA} = \dfrac{\eta}{2\tan(\alpha + \phi)}$, takes the margin as the toggle angle $\alpha$. Its force falls slowly, about 30 percent from 5 to 10 degrees, because the friction angle $\phi$ (about $6.8^\circ$ for a pin friction of 0.12) is added to $\alpha$. In the real linkage the toggle angle is about 8 degrees at a 5-degree margin. Predict whether the simulator's force falls by more or by less than 30 percent.

3. **Sweep the lock margin**
   Set the lock margin to 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 15 degrees. At each value record the clamping force at lock.

4. **Judge release security**
   The simulator cannot show how the handle feels. Use the script's release effort index (the sine of the margin): a larger margin means the handle must be pulled further back before the clamp opens.

5. **Save data**
   Create `data/exp5_lockmargin.csv` with columns: lock_margin_deg, Fpad_sim.
</Steps>

### Python Analysis

```python title="experiment_5_lockmargin.py"
# Toggle Clamp Experiment 5: Lock-Margin Trade-Offs
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import os

os.makedirs('plots', exist_ok=True)

F_handle = 120.0
eta = 0.76
mu = 0.12
phi = np.arctan(mu)

margin = np.linspace(1, 18, 300)        # lock margin past TDC, degrees ~ toggle angle
a = np.deg2rad(margin)
MA = eta / (2 * np.tan(a + phi))
F_pad = MA * F_handle

# A simple release-effort index: the over-centre depth that resists back-driving
release_index = np.sin(a)               # grows with margin

fig, ax1 = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 5))
ax1.plot(margin, F_pad, '-', color='#2A9D8F', lw=2, label='Clamping force')
csv_path = 'data/exp5_lockmargin.csv'
if os.path.exists(csv_path):
    sim = np.genfromtxt(csv_path, delimiter=',', skip_header=1)
    ax1.plot(sim[:, 0], sim[:, 1], 'o', color='#ef4444', ms=6, label='Simulator')
ax1.set_xlabel('Lock margin (deg)')

plt.show()

for m in [2, 5, 10, 15]:
    ma = eta / (2 * np.tan(np.deg2rad(m) + phi))
    print(f"margin={m:2d} deg: F_pad={ma*F_handle:.0f} N, release effort index={np.sin(np.deg2rad(m)):.3f}")
```

### Expected Results

- Simulator clamping force at lock: about 366 N at 2 degrees, 182 N at 5, 89 N at 10, and 55 N at 15
- From 5 to 10 degrees it falls by about half (51 percent). The two-link model in the script gives about 30 percent (217 N to 151 N), so it underestimates the loss in the real linkage
- Release effort rises with margin: a very small margin opens easily, and a large one is secure but stiff
- A very small margin also overloads the main link: about 317 MPa at 2 degrees and 208 MPa at 3 degrees against an allowable of 175 MPa, then 154 MPa at 4 degrees
- A margin around 4 to 6 degrees is a common compromise

## Experiment 6: Selecting a Clamp for a Target Force

<InArticleAd />


The real design task is the inverse one: given a required hold-down force and a comfortable hand effort, which clamp size do you choose? This experiment uses the presets and the A/B comparison to select a clamp for a specified job, and it shows where the simple toggle formula stops being a safe guide.

:::note[Objective]
Given a target clamping force and a maximum comfortable handle force, compare a simple toggle estimate with the simulator's readings for three presets, then select the smallest preset that meets the force target with a Safe stress verdict.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Set the requirement**
   Target hold-down force: 300 N. Maximum comfortable handle force: 100 N. Required MA is therefore at least $300 / 100 = 3$.

2. **Predict**
   A quick estimate multiplies the handle lever ratio $(L_h + L_g) / L_{\text{pad}}$ by the friction-limited toggle factor and the efficiency: $\text{MA} \approx \dfrac{L_h + L_g}{L_{\text{pad}}} \cdot \dfrac{\eta}{2\tan(\alpha + \phi)}$. At a 2.5-degree lock margin this gives about 2.94 for the light-duty preset, 3.15 for medium-duty, and 3.42 for heavy-duty. Note your prediction, then test it: the formula describes an ideal two-link toggle, and the real clamp is a four-bar linkage.

3. **Read the three presets**
   Load the Light-duty preset, set the handle force to 100 N and the lock margin to 2.5 degrees, and keep yield strength at 350 MPa with a safety factor of 2. Record the engineering MA at lock, the clamping force at lock, the transmission angle at lock, the link stress, and the sizing verdict. Click Save as Experiment A, then repeat for the Medium-duty and Heavy-duty presets and compare clamping force and stress on the overlaid charts.

4. **Pick and verify**
   No preset is Safe at 2.5 degrees. For each one, raise the lock margin in 0.5-degree steps until the verdict changes to Safe, and check whether the clamping force is still at least 300 N. Choose the smallest preset that meets both conditions.

5. **Save data**
   Create `data/exp6_selection.csv` with one row for each preset at 2.5 degrees and one for your chosen preset: preset, lock_margin_deg, MA_sim, Fpad_sim, mu_lock_deg, link_stress_MPa, verdict.
</Steps>

### Python Analysis

The script solves the real four-bar geometry of each preset, so its numbers can be checked directly against the simulator panels.

```python title="experiment_6_selection.py"
# Toggle Clamp Experiment 6: Selecting a Clamp for a Target Force
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import os

os.makedirs('plots', exist_ok=True)

target_force = 300.0     # N required at the pad
F_handle = 100.0         # N comfortable hand effort
required_MA = target_force / F_handle
sigma_allow = 350.0 / 2  # MPa, yield 350 with a safety factor of 2
tau_allow = sigma_allow / 2
print(f"Required mechanical advantage: {required_MA:.1f}")

# Preset geometry (mm), pin friction, efficiency, pin diameter, link section
presets = {
    'Light-duty':  dict(Lh=28, Lg=90,  Lm=58,  La=38, Lpad=95,  d=58,  f=0.12, eta=0.78, pin=6,  t=5, w=18),
    'Medium-duty': dict(Lh=40, Lg=110, Lm=80,  La=55, Lpad=110, d=70,  f=0.12, eta=0.76, pin=8,  t=6, w=22),
    'Heavy-duty':  dict(Lh=45, Lg=165, Lm=110, La=72, Lpad=170, d=100, f=0.10, eta=0.80, pin=12, t=8, w=28),
}

def cross(u, v):
    return u[0] * v[1] - u[1] * v[0]

def joints(p, th_deg):
    """Handle joint A and toggle joint B (above the arm pivot) for a handle angle."""
    th = np.deg2rad(th_deg)
    A = np.array([p['Lh'] * np.cos(th), p['Lh'] * np.sin(th)])
    O4 = np.array([p['d'], 0.0])
    gap = O4 - A
    dist = np.hypot(*gap)
    a = (p['Lm']**2 - p['La']**2 + dist**2) / (2 * dist)
    hh = np.sqrt(p['Lm']**2 - a**2)
    B = A + a * gap / dist + hh * np.array([-gap[1], gap[0]]) / dist
    return A, B, O4

def tdc_angle(p):
    """Handle angle where O2, A and B are collinear (bisection)."""
    g = lambda th: cross(*joints(p, th)[:2])
    lo, hi = 1.0, 60.0
    for _ in range(60):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if g(lo) * g(mid) <= 0:
            hi = mid
        else:
            lo = mid
    return 0.5 * (lo + hi)

def at_lock(p, margin_deg):
    A, B, O4 = joints(p, tdc_angle(p) - margin_deg)
    AB = (B - A) / np.hypot(*(B - A))
    arm = B - O4
    lever = (p['Lh'] + p['Lg']) / p['Lpad']
    beta = np.arctan(p['f'])
    # Simple estimate: ideal symmetric two-link toggle, with friction and efficiency
    MA_simple = lever * p['eta'] / (2 * np.tan(np.deg2rad(margin_deg) + beta))
    # Real four-bar: velocity ratio of handle to clamp arm (virtual work)
    MA_rigid = lever * abs(cross(arm, AB) / cross(A, AB))
    # Simulator engineering model: rigid x efficiency, limited by a friction cap
    alpha = np.arccos(np.clip(np.dot(A, AB) / p['Lh'], -1, 1))
    cap = 1 / (2 * np.tan(alpha + beta) * np.tan(np.deg2rad(30)))
    MA_eng = MA_rigid * p['eta'] / (1 + MA_rigid * p['eta'] / cap)
    mu = np.degrees(np.arccos(abs(np.dot(AB, arm)) / p['La']))
    # Main link force, link stress (axial + pin eccentricity bending), pin shear
    F_link = F_handle * (p['Lh'] + p['Lg']) / abs(cross(A, AB))
    sigma = F_link / (p['t'] * p['w']) + 3 * F_link * p['pin'] / (p['w'] * p['t']**2)
    tau = F_link / (np.pi * p['pin']**2 / 4)
    safe = sigma <= sigma_allow and tau <= tau_allow
    return dict(simple=MA_simple, rigid=MA_rigid, eng=MA_eng, mu=mu,
                Fpad=MA_eng * F_handle, sigma=sigma, tau=tau, safe=safe)

print("\nAt a 2.5 degree lock margin and 100 N at the handle:")
for name, p in presets.items():
    r = at_lock(p, 2.5)
    print(f"{name:12s}: simple MA={r['simple']:.2f}, four-bar rigid MA={r['rigid']:.2f}, "
          f"engineering MA={r['eng']:.2f}, F_pad={r['Fpad']:.0f} N, mu={r['mu']:.1f} deg, "
          f"link {r['sigma']:.0f} MPa, pin {r['tau']:.0f} MPa "
          f"[{'Safe' if r['safe'] else 'Over-stressed'}]")

# Sweep the lock margin: where does each preset meet the target with a Safe verdict?
margins = np.arange(2.0, 8.01, 0.5)
plt.figure(figsize=(8, 5))
colors = {'Light-duty': '#E9C46A', 'Medium-duty': '#2A9D8F', 'Heavy-duty': '#264653'}
best = None
print("\nLock margins (0.5 degree steps) that give at least 300 N with a Safe verdict:")
for name, p in presets.items():
    res = [at_lock(p, m) for m in margins]
    F = np.array([r['Fpad'] for r in res])
    ok = np.array([r['safe'] for r in res])
    good = [m for m, r in zip(margins, res) if r['safe'] and r['Fpad'] >= target_force]
    print(f"{name:12s}: {', '.join(f'{m:.1f}' for m in good) if good else 'none'}")
    if good and best is None:
        best = (name, good[0], at_lock(p, good[0]))
    plt.plot(margins, F, '-', color=colors[name], lw=2, label=name)
    plt.plot(margins[~ok], F[~ok], 'x', color='#ef4444', ms=8)
plt.plot([], [], 'x', color='#ef4444', label='Over-stressed')
plt.axhline(target_force, color='#94a3b8', ls='--', label=f'Target {target_force:.0f} N')
plt.xlabel('Lock margin (degrees)'); plt.ylabel('Clamping force at pad (N)')
plt.title('Clamping force at 100 N hand force, three presets')
plt.legend(); plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('plots/exp6_selection.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()

name, m, r = best
print(f"\nChosen preset: {name} at a {m:.1f} degree lock margin")
print(f"F_pad = {r['Fpad']:.0f} N, link stress = {r['sigma']:.0f} MPa (allowable {sigma_allow:.0f} MPa), "
      f"pin shear = {r['tau']:.0f} MPa (allowable {tau_allow:.0f} MPa)")
```

### Expected Results

- At a 2.5-degree lock margin and 100 N at the handle, light-duty (322 N) and heavy-duty (372 N) clear the 300 N target in the simulator; medium-duty (264 N) falls short
- The simple estimate (2.94, 3.15, 3.42) puts medium-duty above light-duty, which is the wrong order. That formula is for an ideal symmetric two-link toggle. It ignores the transmission angle, which takes force from a preset that locks at a small one: medium-duty locks at about 17 degrees, against about 32 and 30 degrees for the other two
- All three presets are Over-stressed at 2.5 degrees: the link stress is 328, 210, and 184 MPa against an allowable of 175 MPa
- The smallest preset that meets 300 N with a Safe verdict is heavy-duty, at a 3-degree lock margin: 333 N at the pad with a link stress of 153 MPa. Medium-duty becomes Safe at 3.5 degrees but then gives only 206 N, and light-duty becomes Safe only at 7 degrees with 153 N
- The larger clamp is chosen here for the strength of its links and pins at the required force, more than for a higher mechanical advantage

Reducing the lock margin raises MA but also raises the link and pin stress, and it runs into the friction ceiling; increasing the handle lever ratio (a longer handle or a shorter pad arm) also raises MA. Which change would you try first, and what physical limit eventually stops each one?
