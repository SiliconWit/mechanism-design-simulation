## How a scissor lift works

A scissor lift is a platform on crossed arms that are joined by a pin at their middle, like a pair of scissors. A car jack, a lifting table in a workshop, and a work platform for reaching a ceiling all use it. An actuator, which is a hydraulic cylinder or a screw, pulls the feet of the arms toward each other. The arms stand up and the platform rises.

```text
          load W
   +-----------------+   platform
    \               /
      \           /
        \       /        each arm has length L
          \   /
            o   <------- crossing pin
          /   \
        /       \
      /  θ        \
    o---------------o    the actuator pulls the feet
    |<----- S ----->|    together with force F
```

The surprise is where the lift needs its force. Near the folded position a small movement of the feet raises the platform a long way, so the actuator does the same work over a shorter distance and needs a larger force, as standing up is hardest from a deep squat. The actuator force is the load, times the number of stages, divided by the tangent of the arm angle:

$$
F = \frac{n\,W_{\text{eff}}}{\tan\theta}
$$

$F$ is the actuator force, $W_{\text{eff}}$ the platform load plus half the weight of the arms, $n$ the number of stages, and $\theta$ the angle of an arm above the horizontal. A small angle has a small tangent, so the force is large: the lift of the first experiment needs 951 N at 30 degrees and 3114 N at 10 degrees. A scissor lift needs its greatest force at its lowest position, where there is the least room for an actuator, so every real lift has a lowest working angle.

:::caution[Simulator only]
A raised lift holds stored energy: if a pin, a hose, or a screw fails, the platform falls. Do these experiments on the simulator. Never overload or tilt a real lift to test it, and never reach between the arms of a raised lift that is not propped.
:::

## Reference

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **L** | Link length: the length of each scissor arm (mm) |
| **$\theta$ (theta)** | Angle of an arm above the horizontal; $0^\circ$ is folded flat |
| **h** | Platform height: $h = nL\sin\theta$ for $n$ stages |
| **S** | Spread: horizontal distance between base pivots, $S = L\cos\theta$ |
| **P** | Platform load (N), spread evenly (a uniform distributed load, UDL) or placed at one point |
| **F** | Actuator force: the force the hydraulic cylinder or lead screw must exert |
| **Stroke** | The distance the actuator travels from the lowest to the highest position |
| **MA** | Mechanical advantage, $P / F$. Below 1, the actuator pushes harder than the load weighs |
| **n** | Number of scissor stages (1 to 3). Each stage adds one full link height |
| **Symmetric** | Both base pivots slide, so the scissor stays centered under the platform |
| **Left-pinned** | One base pivot is fixed (pinned to ground). The actuator force is the same, but the scissor center moves sideways as the lift rises |
| **CoG** | Center of gravity: the point where the whole weight can be taken to act |
| **Tipping angle** | The tilt at which the lift would fall over; larger is safer. The simulator's safety factor is the tipping angle divided by 5 degrees |

## Experiment 1: Baseline Height and Force Profile

<InArticleAd />


An actuator that is too weak for the lowest position cannot start the lift, however easily it holds the load higher up. The height rises with the sine of the arm angle, $h = L\sin\theta$, while the force falls with the tangent. This experiment maps both curves for one lift and shows why a real scissor lift never starts from flat.

:::note[Objective]
Verify the height equation $h = nL\sin\theta$ and the actuator force equation $F = \dfrac{n\,W_{\text{eff}}}{\tan\theta}$ for a horizontal-base actuator (a cylinder that joins the two base pivots). Explain why the actuator force rises so fast at low angles.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Configure the simulator**
   Set L = 300 mm, Base Width = 400 mm, 1 stage, symmetric configuration, horizontal-base actuator, Platform Load = 500 N, Link Mass = 5 kg. Set operating range to 10 to 80 degrees.

2. **Predict**
   Calculate by hand at $\theta = 30^\circ$. The height should be $300\sin 30^\circ = 150\ \text{mm}$. The effective load is the platform load plus half the weight of the two arms: $500 + \dfrac{2(1)(5)(9.81)}{2} = 549.05\ \text{N}$. The actuator force should be $\dfrac{549.05}{\tan 30^\circ} = 951.0\ \text{N}$.

3. **Run the experiment**
   Click "Run Full Experiment." Open the Platform Height tab and the Actuator Force tab. The height curve is steep at low angles and flattens near the top. The force curve is very high at low angles and almost flat above 45 degrees.

4. **Record key values**
   Read the info bar at θ = 10, 20, 30, 45, 60, and 80 degrees (use the theta slider). Record h, F, and MA at each angle.

5. **Save data**
   Create `data/exp1_baseline.csv` with columns: theta, height_sim, force_sim, MA_sim.

6. **Export charts**
   Download the Height and Actuator Force charts as PNG files to your plots folder.
</Steps>

### Python Analysis

```python title="experiment_1_baseline.py"
# Scissor Lift Experiment 1: Baseline Height and Force Profile
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import os

# STEP 1: Load simulator data (if available)
csv_path = 'data/exp1_baseline.csv'
sim_data = None
if os.path.exists(csv_path):
    sim_data = np.genfromtxt(csv_path, delimiter=',', skip_header=1)
    print(f"Loaded {len(sim_data)} data points from {csv_path}")

# STEP 2: Analytical calculations
L = 300       # mm
n = 1         # stages
P = 500       # N platform load
m_link = 5    # kg per link
g = 9.81      # m/s^2

total_link_weight = 2 * n * m_link * g
effective_load = P + total_link_weight / 2

theta_deg = np.linspace(5, 85, 200)
theta_rad = np.deg2rad(theta_deg)

height = n * L * np.sin(theta_rad)
force = effective_load * n / np.tan(theta_rad)
ma = P / force

# STEP 3: Plot
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(15, 5))

# Height vs angle
axes[0].plot(theta_deg, height, 'b-', linewidth=2, label='Analytical: h = nL sin(θ)')
if sim_data is not None:
    axes[0].plot(sim_data[:, 0], sim_data[:, 1], 'ro', markersize=6, label='Simulator')
axes[0].set_xlabel('θ (degrees)')
axes[0].set_ylabel('Height (mm)')
axes[0].set_title('Platform Height vs Angle')
axes[0].legend()
axes[0].grid(True, alpha=0.3)

# Force vs angle
axes[1].plot(theta_deg, force, 'r-', linewidth=2, label='Analytical: F = W/tan θ')
if sim_data is not None:
    axes[1].plot(sim_data[:, 0], sim_data[:, 2], 'bo', markersize=6, label='Simulator')
axes[1].set_xlabel('θ (degrees)')
axes[1].set_ylabel('Force (N)')
axes[1].set_title('Actuator Force vs Angle')
axes[1].legend()
axes[1].grid(True, alpha=0.3)
axes[1].set_ylim(0, min(4000, max(force) * 1.1))

# MA vs angle
axes[2].plot(theta_deg, ma, 'g-', linewidth=2, label='MA = P / F')
if sim_data is not None:
    axes[2].plot(sim_data[:, 0], sim_data[:, 3], 'ko', markersize=6, label='Simulator')
axes[2].set_xlabel('θ (degrees)')
axes[2].set_ylabel('Mechanical Advantage')
axes[2].set_title('Mechanical Advantage vs Angle')
axes[2].legend()
axes[2].grid(True, alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('plots/exp1_baseline.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()
print(f"\nAt θ=30°: h={n*L*np.sin(np.deg2rad(30)):.1f} mm, F={effective_load/np.tan(np.deg2rad(30)):.1f} N")
print(f"At θ=10°: F={effective_load/np.tan(np.deg2rad(10)):.1f} N (note the spike)")
print(f"At θ=80°: F={effective_load/np.tan(np.deg2rad(80)):.1f} N (very low)")
```

### Expected Results

- Height rises along a sine curve from about 52 mm (at 10 degrees) to 295 mm (at 80 degrees)
- Actuator force rises sharply below 20 degrees and passes 3100 N at 10 degrees, more than six times the platform load
- At 45 degrees, the force equals the effective load (about 549 N, so MA is about 0.91); MA reaches 1.0 near 48 degrees
- Mechanical advantage rises with the angle over the whole range: poor at low angles, high at high angles
- The simulator and analytical values should match within 1%

## Experiment 2: Comparing Actuator Types

<InArticleAd />


Where the actuator is mounted decides how hard it must push and how far it must travel. The simulator has three mountings: horizontal-base (a hydraulic cylinder between the base pivots), horizontal-center (a lead screw, which is a threaded rod, that pulls the arm midpoints together), and diagonal (a cylinder from the base to a point partway along an arm). A mounting chosen only because it packs neatly can double the force on the actuator.

:::note[Objective]
Compare the actuator force, mechanical advantage, and power curves for all three actuator types on the same mechanism geometry. Identify which type requires the least peak force and which has the smoothest force curve.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Set common parameters**
   L = 300 mm, 1 stage, symmetric, UDL, P = 500 N, Link Mass = 5 kg, range 10 to 80 degrees.

2. **Run horizontal-base experiment**
   Select "Horizontal Base" actuator. Click "Save as Experiment A." Note the peak force, min force, and actuator stroke from the results summary.

3. **Run horizontal-center experiment**
   Switch to "Horizontal Center (lead screw)" actuator. Click "Run Full Experiment." The overlay shows both curves. The lead screw curve should sit at twice the height of the first one at every angle.

4. **Record and clear**
   Record the horizontal-center results. Click "Clear" on Experiment A.

5. **Run diagonal experiment**
   Save horizontal-center as new Experiment A. Switch to "Diagonal" actuator. Run again to compare.

6. **Collect data**
   Create `data/exp2_actuator_types.csv` with one row for each actuator type: actuator_type, peak_force, min_force, stroke, peak_power.
</Steps>

### Python Analysis

```python title="experiment_2_actuator_types.py"
# Scissor Lift Experiment 2: Actuator Type Comparison
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

L = 300; n = 1; P = 500; m = 5; g = 9.81
W_eff = P + 2 * n * m * g / 2
v_act = 50  # mm/s

theta = np.linspace(10, 80, 200)
theta_rad = np.deg2rad(theta)
sin_t = np.sin(theta_rad)
cos_t = np.cos(theta_rad)
tan_t = np.tan(theta_rad)

# Horizontal base: F = W / tan θ
F_base = W_eff * n / tan_t

# Horizontal center: F = 2 W / tan θ
F_center = 2 * W_eff * n / tan_t

# Diagonal: F = W * n * L * cos θ / |dD/dθ|
def diag_deriv(L, t):
    f = 0.09 * np.cos(t)**2 + 0.49 * np.sin(t)**2
    return L * 0.40 * np.sin(2*t) / (2 * np.sqrt(f))

dDdT = np.abs(diag_deriv(L, theta_rad))
F_diag = np.where(dDdT > 1e-10, W_eff * n * L * cos_t / dDdT, 0)

# Power = F * v_actuator (in Watts)
P_base = np.abs(F_base) * v_act / 1000
P_center = np.abs(F_center) * v_act / 1000
P_diag = np.abs(F_diag) * v_act / 1000

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 5))

axes[0].plot(theta, F_base, 'b-', linewidth=2, label='Horizontal Base')
axes[0].plot(theta, F_center, 'r-', linewidth=2, label='Horizontal Center')
axes[0].plot(theta, F_diag, 'g-', linewidth=2, label='Diagonal')
axes[0].set_xlabel('θ (degrees)')
axes[0].set_ylabel('Actuator Force (N)')
axes[0].set_title('Force Comparison by Actuator Type')
axes[0].legend()
axes[0].grid(True, alpha=0.3)
axes[0].set_ylim(0, 6500)

axes[1].plot(theta, P_base, 'b-', linewidth=2, label='Horizontal Base')
axes[1].plot(theta, P_center, 'r-', linewidth=2, label='Horizontal Center')
axes[1].plot(theta, P_diag, 'g-', linewidth=2, label='Diagonal')
axes[1].set_xlabel('θ (degrees)')
axes[1].set_ylabel('Power (W)')
axes[1].set_title('Power Comparison by Actuator Type')
axes[1].legend()
axes[1].grid(True, alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('plots/exp2_actuator_types.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()

print(f"Horizontal Base: Peak F = {max(F_base):.0f} N, Min F = {min(F_base):.0f} N")
print(f"Horizontal Center: Peak F = {max(F_center):.0f} N, Min F = {min(F_center):.0f} N")
print(f"Diagonal: Peak F = {max(F_diag):.0f} N, Min F = {min(F_diag):.0f} N")
print(f"\nHorizontal Center requires {max(F_center)/max(F_base):.1f}x the peak force of Horizontal Base")
```

### Expected Results

- Horizontal-base peaks at about 3114 N at 10 degrees and falls to under 100 N at 80 degrees, because the actuator spans the full spread and has the longest stroke (243 mm)
- Horizontal-center (lead screw) requires twice the force of horizontal-base at every angle (about 6228 N at 10 degrees), because it has half the stroke (122 mm) for the same lift
- Diagonal force curve has a different shape: it has the lowest peak of the three (about 2525 N at 10 degrees) but drops off far more gradually. It crosses above the horizontal-base curve near 21 degrees and is still near 960 N at 80 degrees
- All three raise the load through the same height, so all three do the same work. A lower force is always paid for with a longer stroke
- Power curves differ because the actuator speed is fixed at 50 mm/s: a mounting that needs more force at that speed gives more power and raises the platform faster

### Design Question

You are designing a lab jack that must be very compact when collapsed. The diagonal actuator has the smallest collapsed footprint. But the horizontal-center uses a lead screw which is self-locking (holds position without power). Which would you choose for a precision lab instrument, and why?

## Experiment 3: Symmetric vs Left-Pinned Configuration

<InArticleAd />


The simulator offers two joint arrangements: symmetric (both base pivots slide) and left-pinned (one pivot is fixed to the ground). It is easy to assume that the pinned arrangement makes one actuator do twice the work. It does not. The actuator joins the same two base pivots in both, so the force is the same. What changes is where the scissor sits under the platform. This experiment shows both facts, and when each configuration is the better engineering choice.

:::note[Objective]
Show that actuator force, height, and stroke are identical for symmetric and left-pinned configurations with identical loading, and explain why using work in equals work out. Then measure how far the scissor center travels sideways in the left-pinned configuration and what that does to the tipping margin.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Configure symmetric baseline**
   L = 300 mm, 1 stage, symmetric, horizontal-base, P = 1000 N, Link Mass = 5 kg, range 10 to 70 degrees.

2. **Run and save as Experiment A**
   Click "Run Full Experiment," then "Save as Experiment A."

3. **Switch to left-pinned**
   Change Joint Arrangement to "Left-Pinned." Click "Run Full Experiment." The force curve overlays on the previous one.

4. **Compare force charts**
   Open the Actuator Force tab. The two curves lie exactly on top of each other, which means that fixing one pivot does not change the work the actuator must do. The peak force is 5949.5 N at 10 degrees in both configurations.

5. **Watch the scissor center**
   Play the animation in each configuration. In symmetric, the crossing pin stays on the center line. In left-pinned, it moves toward the fixed pin as the lift rises. Read S on the info bar at 10 degrees and at a higher angle: the center has moved by half of the change in S.

6. **Compare stability**
   Open the Stability tab. The simulator reports the same tipping angle in both configurations, because it always places the load over the scissor center. A real load stays in the middle of the platform while the center moves away under it. To model that, keep left-pinned, change Load Distribution to "Point Load," and set Load Position to 40 mm (the center travel at 45 degrees is about 42 mm, and 40 mm is the nearest slider step). Run again and read the tipping angle at 45 degrees.

7. **Record data**
   Save `data/exp3_config_compare.csv` with: theta, F_symmetric, F_left_pinned, center_travel, tipping_centered, tipping_shifted.
</Steps>

### Python Analysis

```python title="experiment_3_configurations.py"
# Scissor Lift Experiment 3: Symmetric vs Left-Pinned
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

L = 300; n = 1; P = 1000; m = 5; g = 9.81
theta_min = 10
W_eff = P + 2 * n * m * g / 2

theta = np.linspace(theta_min, 70, 200)
theta_rad = np.deg2rad(theta)
tan_t = np.tan(theta_rad)
cos_t = np.cos(theta_rad)
sin_t = np.sin(theta_rad)

# The actuator joins the two base pivots in both configurations.
# Work in = work out: F * d(L cos θ) = W_eff * d(n L sin θ)
F_sym = W_eff * n / tan_t
F_lp = W_eff * n / tan_t

# Left-pinned only: the scissor center moves toward the fixed pin
halfSpread = (L / 2) * cos_t
travel = (L / 2) * (np.cos(np.deg2rad(theta_min)) - cos_t)

# Stability: tipping angle = atan(margin / cogY)
height = n * L * sin_t
total_weight = P + 2 * n * m * g
link_cog_y = height / 2  # approximate
cog_y = (P * height + 2*n*m*g * link_cog_y) / total_weight
tip_centered = np.degrees(np.arctan(halfSpread / cog_y))

# Load fixed in the middle of the platform while the center moves away
cog_x = P * travel / total_weight
margin = np.maximum(halfSpread - cog_x, 0)
tip_shifted = np.degrees(np.arctan(margin / cog_y))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 5))

axes[0].plot(theta, F_sym, 'b-', linewidth=3, label='Symmetric')
axes[0].plot(theta, F_lp, 'r--', linewidth=1.5, label='Left-Pinned')
axes[0].set_xlabel('θ (degrees)')
axes[0].set_ylabel('Actuator Force (N)')
axes[0].set_title('Force: Symmetric vs Left-Pinned')
axes[0].legend()
axes[0].grid(True, alpha=0.3)

axes[1].plot(theta, tip_centered, 'g-', linewidth=2, label='Load over scissor center')
axes[1].plot(theta, tip_shifted, 'm-', linewidth=2, label='Left-pinned, load fixed on platform')
axes[1].axhline(y=5, color='r', linestyle='--', label='5° design limit')
axes[1].set_xlabel('θ (degrees)')
axes[1].set_ylabel('Tipping Angle (degrees)')
axes[1].set_title('Stability vs Angle')
axes[1].legend()
axes[1].grid(True, alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('plots/exp3_configurations.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()

i45 = np.argmin(np.abs(theta - 45))
print(f"Force ratio (left-pinned / symmetric) = {F_lp[50]/F_sym[50]:.2f} (should be 1.00)")
print(f"Peak force at θ={theta_min}°: {F_sym[0]:.1f} N in both configurations")
print(f"Center travel at θ=45°: {travel[i45]:.1f} mm, at θ=70°: {travel[-1]:.1f} mm")
print(f"Tipping angle at θ=45°: {tip_centered[i45]:.1f}° centered, {tip_shifted[i45]:.1f}° with the load fixed on the platform")
print(f"Load reaches the sliding pivot at θ = {theta[np.argmax(margin == 0)]:.0f}°")
```

### Expected Results

- Actuator force is identical at every angle (ratio 1.00): about 3915 N at 15 degrees, 1049 N at 45 degrees, and 606 N at 60 degrees in both configurations
- This is because the actuator joins the same two base pivots in both. The work it does over a small change in spread must equal the work done on the load over the matching change in height, and fixing one pivot to the ground changes neither
- Height, stroke (192.8 mm), mechanical advantage, power, and energy are also identical
- The position is what differs: in left-pinned the scissor center travels about 42 mm toward the fixed pin by 45 degrees and 96 mm by 70 degrees (half of the 192.8 mm spread range). In symmetric it does not move
- A load that stays in the middle of the platform therefore ends up off center over the supports in left-pinned. At 45 degrees the tipping angle falls from 27.6 degrees to about 19 degrees, and near 62 degrees the load reaches the sliding pivot, so the fixed pin must hold the platform down

### Design Question

A car jack must be operated by hand with a screw mechanism. The hand force is the same for symmetric and left-pinned, so the choice depends on other things. Which configuration keeps the load over the middle of the supports as the jack rises, and which is easier to anchor on uneven ground? What about a warehouse lift that uses a hydraulic pump and carries pallets placed in the middle of the platform?

## Experiment 4: Multi-Stage Height Multiplication

<InArticleAd />


When a lift must reach higher, the cheap answer is to stack another stage on top. Each stage adds one full link height, but the actuator force grows at least as fast, and the lift becomes easier to tip. This experiment measures all three effects and shows why most practical scissor lifts use 1 to 3 stages.

:::note[Objective]
Compare the height, actuator force, and stability of 1-stage, 2-stage, and 3-stage scissor lifts with the same link length. Verify that height scales as n but force also scales as n.
:::

<Steps>
1. **Configure 1-stage baseline**
   L = 300 mm, symmetric, horizontal-base, P = 500 N, Link Mass = 5 kg, range 10 to 70 degrees.

2. **Run and save as Experiment A**
   Record max height, peak force, and min tipping angle from the results summary.

3. **Switch to 2 stages**
   Change Number of Stages to 2. Click "Run Full Experiment." The overlay shows both.

4. **Record 2-stage results**
   Note the new max height (should be 2x), peak force (a little more than 2x, because the second stage adds link weight), and min tipping angle (should be lower).

5. **Clear and test 3 stages**
   Clear Experiment A, save 2-stage as new A, switch to 3 stages, run again.

6. **Collect all data**
   Save `data/exp4_stages.csv` with one row for each stage count: stages, max_height, peak_force, min_tipping_angle, actuator_stroke.
</Steps>

```python title="experiment_4_stages.py"
# Scissor Lift Experiment 4: Multi-Stage Comparison
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

L = 300; P = 500; m = 5; g = 9.81

- The total actuator force stays approximately the same at any offset in the symmetric configuration, because the total vertical load has not changed

<InArticleAd />

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

L = 500; n = 1; P = 3000; m = 10; g = 9.81; v_act = 30
total_link_weight = 2 * n * m * g
W_eff = P + total_link_weight / 2

theta = np.linspace(10, 70, 200)
theta_rad = np.deg2rad(theta)

height = n * L * np.sin(theta_rad)
h_min = n * L * np.sin(np.deg2rad(10))

PE_links = total_link_weight * delta_h / 2000
PE_total = PE_platform + PE_links

# Actuator force
force = W_eff * n / np.tan(theta_rad)

# Power = F * v_actuator (W)
power = np.abs(force) * v_act / 1000

# Cumulative work (numerical integration)
work = np.zeros_like(theta)
for i in range(1, len(theta)):
    dh = height[i] - height[i-1]
    work[i] = work[i-1] + W_eff * abs(dh) / 1000

axes[0].set_title('Energy Conservation'); axes[0].legend(); axes[0].grid(True, alpha=0.3)

axes[1].plot(theta, power, 'g-', linewidth=2)
peak_idx = np.argmax(power)
axes[1].axvline(x=theta[peak_idx], color='r', linestyle='--', alpha=0.5)
axes[1].annotate(f'Peak: {power[peak_idx]:.1f} W at {theta[peak_idx]:.0f}°',

axes[1].set_xlabel('θ (degrees)'); axes[1].set_ylabel('Power (W)')

axes[2].plot(theta, force, 'r-', linewidth=2)
axes[2].set_xlabel('θ (degrees)'); axes[2].set_ylabel('Actuator Force (N)')
axes[2].set_title('Actuator Force'); axes[2].grid(True, alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('plots/exp7_energy.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()

print(f"Total energy to raise: {PE_total[-1]:.1f} J")
print(f"Peak power: {max(power):.1f} W at θ = {theta[np.argmax(power)]:.0f}°")
print(f"Peak force: {max(force):.0f} N at θ = {theta[0]:.0f}°")
print(f"Energy conservation: work/PE = {work[-1]/PE_total[-1]:.4f}")

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

L = 300; n = 1; m = 5; g = 9.81

loads = [500, 1000, 2000, 5000]
theta = np.linspace(10, 80, 200)
theta_rad = np.deg2rad(theta)

# Link cross-section assumptions (same as simulator)
b = L / 20   # width = 15 mm
h = L / 10   # height = 30 mm
I = b * h**3 / 12  # second moment of area
c = h / 2    # distance to neutral axis

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 6))

for idx, P in enumerate(loads):
    W_eff = P + 2 * n * m * g / 2
    F_arm = W_eff / (2 * n)    # load per arm
    M = F_arm * L / 4           # bending moment at quarter span
    stress = M * c / I          # N/mm^2 is already MPa (constant with angle in this model)

    ax.axhline(y=stress, color=colors[idx], linewidth=2, label=f'P = {P} N ({stress:.1f} MPa)')

ax.axhline(y=250, color='red', linestyle='--', linewidth=1.5, label='Steel yield (250 MPa)')
ax.axhline(y=270, color='blue', linestyle='--', linewidth=1.5, label='Al 6061 yield (270 MPa)')

ax.set_ylabel('Bending Stress (MPa)')
ax.set_title('Link Bending Stress vs Load')
ax.legend()
ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
ax.set_ylim(0, 300)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('plots/exp8_stress.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()

print("Cross-section: b = {:.0f} mm, h = {:.0f} mm, I = {:.0f} mm^4".format(b, h, I))
for P in loads:
    W_eff = P + 2 * n * m * g / 2
    F_arm = W_eff / (2 * n)
    M = F_arm * L / 4
    stress = M * c / I
    sf = 250 / stress
    print(f"P = {P} N: stress = {stress:.1f} MPa, safety factor (steel) = {sf:.1f}")
```

### Expected Results

- Bending stress is approximately constant across the angle range (the simplified model treats it as a statically loaded beam)

- A 5000 N load gives about 84 MPa, a safety factor of about 3 against steel yield for this geometry
- The safety factor against yield falls in proportion as the load rises

        peak_F = W_eff * p['n'] * p['L'] * np.cos(t) / dDdT

    results.append((name, max_h, peak_F, p['config'], p['act'], p['n']))
    print(f"{name:20s}: max h = {max_h:7.0f} mm, peak F = {peak_F:8.0f} N, {p['config']:5s}/{p['act']}")

# Bar chart comparison
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(14, 6))
names = [r[0] for r in results]
heights = [r[1] for r in results]
forces = [r[2] for r in results]
x = np.arange(len(names))

axes[0].set_title('Maximum Platform Height')

### Expected Results

- Aerial Platform has the largest max height (over 3300 mm with 3 stages and $L = 1200\ \text{mm}$)
- Loading Dock has the highest peak actuator force of the horizontal-base presets (about $93{,}700\ \text{N}$ at its 5-degree minimum angle); the Auto Hoist diagonal actuator is higher still (about $96{,}500\ \text{N}$)
- Lab Jack has the smallest forces (about 410 N) and the most compact dimensions
- Car Jack uses left-pinned because it needs a fixed base point for stability on uneven ground
- Lead screw actuators (Lab Jack, Electric Table) are self-locking: they hold position without hydraulic pressure
- Diagonal actuators (Auto Hoist, Work Table) offer a compact folded profile but complex force curves
- The number of stages correlates strongly with required height: 3-stage for tall lifts, 1-stage for compact ones
